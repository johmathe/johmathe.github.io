NeurIPS 2026

: Selective \(G\)-Bispectra Made Practical

Fast, exact, lossless symmetry invariants for PyTorch.

Johan Mathe, Adele Myers Lantow, Simon Mataigne and Nina Miolane · Paper · Code

Turn a digit on a sphere, and a network trained on upright digits will often read another digit. The usual fixes either train on many turned copies or throw information away. The bispectrum is a third way. Its features don't change when the input is rotated, shifted or flipped, and for generic signals they determine the input up to that transformation. That second property, completeness, is what we mean by lossless. It is proven for finite groups and for images on a disk, and on the sphere it is a conjecture that every signal we tested supports.

bispectrum is a PyTorch library that computes these invariants in seven modules, from cyclic shifts of a ring to rotations of the sphere, in a fraction of a millisecond per batch on a GPU. These notes follow our NeurIPS 2026 talk in five parts. The figures run live in your browser, and the numbered steps in their captions replay the talk's demos.

Why invariance is hard

A digit on its side

Take a 2 from MNIST, drawn on a sphere, and a small CNN that reads the sphere as an image. The CNN was trained on upright digits only, and on this upright 2 it is right. Turn the digit on its side and it reads an 8. Let the digit spin and the answer changes with the pose.

The CNN is not a bad model. On upright test digits it is right 99.1% of the time, the best of the three models on this page, and on rotated ones it falls to 13.3%, close to chance. Nothing in its weights says that a turned 2 is still a 2, so the invariance has to come from somewhere else.

Two standard fixes

There are two standard ways to get invariance. The first is data augmentation. We train on turned copies of every digit, so that the network sees each pose. Training takes longer, by a factor that grows with the number of poses, and what the network learns is invariant only approximately, for the poses and the digits it was shown.

The second is invariant pooling. A group convolution applies each filter in every pose and keeps only the largest response, or the signal is reduced to its power spectrum. Both are invariant by construction. Both also throw information away, and two different digits can pool to the same value.

The power spectrum

On the sphere a signal is a sum of spherical harmonics, waves grouped by degree, much as a periodic signal is a sum of frequencies. The power spectrum keeps one number per degree, the energy of the signal at that degree, and a rotation leaves every one of them unchanged. For the digits here that is 13 numbers. A small MLP trained on them, again on upright digits only, gives the same answer in every pose.

For this 2 the answer is a 3 (70%), at every angle. The power spectrum keeps how much of the signal sits at each scale and drops how the pieces line up, which is most of what makes a 2 a 2. Across the test set the model is right on 77.6% of upright digits and 77.4% of rotated ones. Its invariance is exact, and it still fails, because its features forget much more than the pose.

What the bispectrum preserves

Invariant and complete

What we want is both properties at once. Let's write \(g \cdot x\) for a signal \(x\) transformed by an element \(g\) of a group \(G\), here a rotation. We call \(g\) the signal's pose, and the set of all its transformed copies its orbit. An invariant \(\Phi\) is constant on orbits, so every pose of the 2 gets the same features. It is complete if it also tells orbits apart, so that a 7, in any pose, gets different ones,

\[ \Phi(x) = \Phi(y) \iff y = g \cdot x \text{ for some } g \in G. \]

An invariant has to forget the pose, and a complete one forgets nothing else. Following the paper, we call the vector \(\Phi(x)\) the signal's invariant descriptor.

Counting dimensions gives a lower bound on the size of a complete invariant that varies smoothly with the signal. The digits here are kept as 169 coefficients. A digit with no rotational symmetry, which is almost every digit, has a three-dimensional orbit, so the orbits form a space of 166 dimensions, and a smooth complete invariant needs at least that many numbers. Max pooling over 8 rotations keeps one response out of 8, and the power spectrum keeps 13 numbers.

Triple products on a ring

Products of Fourier coefficients do much better, and they are easiest to see on a ring. Let's take a periodic function \(r(\theta)\), drawn as a closed curve with radius \(r(\theta)\) at angle \(\theta\), and write it as a mean radius \(c_0\) plus 8 waves,

\[ r(\theta) = c_0 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{8} |c_k| \cos(k\theta - \varphi_k). \]

The complex numbers \(c_k = |c_k|\, e^{i\varphi_k}\) are the Fourier coefficients of \(r\), conjugated so that a turn advances the phases. Rotating the curve by \(\alpha\) multiplies each coefficient by a phase,

\[ c_k \mapsto e^{ik\alpha}\, c_k, \]

so \(\varphi_k\) advances by \(k\alpha\) and the magnitudes don't change. The power spectrum here is \(|c_k|^2\). It drops the phases, which makes it invariant and also very far from complete. In step 2 of the figure below, a scramble keeps every \(|c_k|\), draws new phases at random, and gives a different curve with the same power spectrum. A rotation moves the phases \(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_8\) along a line, while a scramble can move them anywhere.

Phases add under multiplication, so a product of coefficients whose frequencies sum to zero doesn't change. The smallest interesting ones are triple products,

\[ \beta_{k_1, k_2} = c_{k_1}\, c_{k_2}\, \overline{c_{k_1 + k_2}} \;\mapsto\; e^{i(k_1 + k_2 - (k_1 + k_2))\alpha}\, \beta_{k_1, k_2} = \beta_{k_1, k_2}. \]

The turn cancels, and what survives in the phase of \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is \(\varphi_{k_1} + \varphi_{k_2} - \varphi_{k_1 + k_2}\), a relation between three phases that a rotation preserves and a scramble doesn't. The table of all \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is the bispectrum, so called because it has two frequency indices where the power spectrum has one. Its row \(k_1 = 0\) is \(c_0 |c_k|^2\), the power spectrum again, so the bispectrum knows everything the power spectrum does.

The figure draws the entries with \(k_1 \le k_2\) and \(k_1 + k_2 \le 8\) by their phases, each under the column of \(k_2\). Its second panel is the plane of \((\varphi_1, \varphi_2)\), in which a rotation moves the signal's point along a line of slope 2.

The library's logo is this triangle of three coefficients whose phases cancel under rotation, set in the round counter of a b.

Any group

Nothing in that argument is specific to the ring. What made it work is that the Fourier basis turns the group action into one phase per coefficient. For a general compact group \(G\) the action can't always be split that finely, but it can be split into blocks. The blocks are the irreducible representations of \(G\), or irreps, unitary matrix representations that cannot be split into smaller ones. Irreps play the role of frequencies. The Fourier coefficient \(\hat f(\rho)\) at an irrep \(\rho\) of dimension \(d\) is a \(d \times d\) matrix, and transforming the signal by \(h\) multiplies it on the right by the unitary matrix \(\rho(h)^\dagger\).

The product of two coefficients no longer turns like a single frequency. It is a tensor product, which transforms by \(\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2\) and splits into irreps through a fixed unitary change of basis \(C_{\rho_1, \rho_2}\), the matrix of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. The \(G\)-bispectrum multiplies the product, in that basis, by the conjugate coefficients at the irreps it contains,

\[ \beta_{\rho_1, \rho_2} = \big[\hat f(\rho_1) \otimes \hat f(\rho_2)\big]\, C_{\rho_1, \rho_2} \Big[\bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \hat f(\rho)^\dagger\Big]\, C_{\rho_1, \rho_2}^\dagger. \]

The transformation picked up by the first factor is undone by the conjugates in the middle, as on the ring. There every irrep is \(1 \times 1\), \(C = 1\), and the formula is \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) again, up to conjugation. The same recipe, with its own irreps and Clebsch–Gordan tables, gives the bispectrum of every group in the library.

The \(G\)-bispectrum is invariant on every one of these groups. Kakarala (1992) proved that on a finite group it is also complete, up to a shift, when every Fourier coefficient is nonsingular. We call such signals generic. On the ring the condition is that every \(c_k\) is nonzero, and in both cases the exceptions form a set of measure zero.

Fold-out · skippableIrreps and Clebsch–Gordan coefficients on a polygon

The smallest groups with irreps larger than \(1 \times 1\) are the dihedral groups \(D_n\), the \(n\) turns and \(n\) flips of a regular \(n\)-gon. They are generated by a turn \(a\) and a flip \(x\) with \(x a x = a^{-1}\), so turning and then flipping is not the same as flipping and then turning. Two irreps of \(D_n\) are \(1 \times 1\) (four when \(n\) is even) and send each element to \(\pm 1\). The rest are \(2 \times 2\),

\[ \rho_k(a) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} & -\sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} \\ \sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} & \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_k(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad 0 < k < n/2. \]

These two matrices don't commute, so no change of basis diagonalises both, and a shift mixes the columns of each \(2 \times 2\) coefficient. For a finite group the coefficients are \(\hat f(\rho) = \sum_{g \in G} f(g)\, \rho(g)^\dagger\), and they hold exactly as many numbers as the signal, since \(\sum_\rho d_\rho^2 = |G|\).

The tensor product \(\rho_i \otimes \rho_j\) of two \(2 \times 2\) irreps splits into \(\rho_{i+j} \oplus \rho_{i-j}\). Indices are read modulo \(n\) and up to sign, and a block whose index is a multiple of \(n/2\) splits further into two \(1 \times 1\) irreps. For \(D_5\) the splits are these.

On the ring each cell would hold the single frequency \(k_1 + k_2\). The Clebsch–Gordan matrix performs the split,

\[ C_{\rho_1, \rho_2}^\dagger\, (\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(g)\, C_{\rho_1, \rho_2} = \bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \rho(g). \]

Under a shift by \(h\) the first factor of \(\beta_{\rho_1, \rho_2}\) picks up \((\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(h)^\dagger = C \big[\bigoplus \rho(h)^\dagger\big] C^\dagger\) on the right, the block-diagonal factor picks up \(\bigoplus \rho(h)\) on the left, and the two cancel.

The sphere

On the sphere, under SO(3), the irreps are indexed by a degree \(\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and the matching basis functions are the spherical harmonics \(Y_\ell^m\), \(-\ell \le m \le \ell\). The coefficients of degree \(\ell\) form a vector \(\mathbf F_\ell = (a_\ell^{-\ell}, \dots, a_\ell^{\ell}) \in \mathbb C^{2\ell + 1}\), and a rotation \(R\) acts on it by a unitary matrix of size \(2\ell + 1\), the Wigner D-matrix \(D^\ell(R)\),

\[ \mathbf F_\ell \mapsto D^\ell(R)\, \mathbf F_\ell. \]

Degrees never mix, but the coefficients within a degree do, as the figure below shows. In practice a signal is kept up to some degree \(L\), its band-limit, which leaves \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients. The power spectrum is \(\|\mathbf F_\ell\|^2\), one number per degree. The spherical bispectrum projects the product of two degrees onto a third with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and pairs it with that degree's coefficients,

\[ \beta_{\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell} = \sum_{m_1 + m_2 = m} C^{\ell m}_{\ell_1 m_1 \ell_2 m_2} \; a_{\ell_1}^{m_1}\, a_{\ell_2}^{m_2}\, \overline{a_\ell^{m}}, \]

one number for each triple of degrees with \(|\ell_1 - \ell_2| \le \ell \le \ell_1 + \ell_2\). The projected product and \(\mathbf F_\ell\) transform by the same unitary \(D^\ell(R)\), so their inner product is invariant.

Kakarala's theorem does not cover the sphere. The sphere is a space that SO(3) acts on, not the group itself, so its Fourier coefficients are the vectors \(\mathbf F_\ell\) rather than square matrices, and a theorem that needs nonsingular matrices doesn't apply (Kondor, 2007). On the sphere, generic just means outside a set of measure zero, and part 3 says what we know about completeness there.

Back to the 2

Now let's give the sphere's bispectrum to the same kind of small MLP, trained, like the others, on upright digits only. Its descriptor is 254 invariants of the digit at \(L = 12\), mostly triple products, picked as part 3 explains. On its side the 2 is read as a 2, and as it spins it stays one. Across the test set this model is right on 95.7% of upright digits and 95.7% of rotated ones.

Rebuilding the Earth

Invariance is easy to check, by turning the signal. Completeness is harder, since it claims that nothing but the pose is lost. One way to test it is to rebuild a signal from its invariants alone. Here is the Earth, roughly, as its land mask in spherical harmonics up to degree 20. That is 441 coefficients, enough to make out the continents if you squint. Its power spectrum is 21 numbers, and random globes with the same 21 numbers look nothing like the Earth.

The 638 invariants in the lower panel are sums of products of three or four coefficients, chosen so that a rotation leaves every one unchanged. From them alone, a numerical solver rebuilds the land mask in a pose of its own, since the pose is exactly what invariants forget. Lined up with the original, the rebuilt globe matches it to about 12 digits.

One rebuilt globe is evidence, not a proof. Which invariants to keep, and what is proven about them, are the subject of part 3.

How selective bispectra reduce complexity

Cost

Out of the box the bispectrum is big. It has an entry for every pair of irreps, so on a finite group it holds \(O(|G|^2)\) scalars, quadratic in the size of the signal. A 32 × 32 image under cyclic shifts has 1,024 pixels and a full bispectrum of 524,800 entries. At 256 × 256 the FFT gives 65,536 coefficients and the bispectrum 2,147,516,416 entries, about 17 GB at 8 bytes each (our arithmetic), per image and per forward pass. The bispectrum has been known since Tukey's work in 1953, and its size is a large part of why networks have rarely used it.

A chain on the ring

Most of those entries are redundant. On the ring, \(c_0\) is real for a real signal, so \(\beta_{0,0} = c_0^3\) gives \(c_0\) and \(\beta_{0,1} = c_0 |c_1|^2\) gives \(|c_1|\). Each entry \(\beta_{1,k} = c_1 c_k \overline{c_{k+1}}\) then gives the next coefficient,

\[ c_{k+1} = \overline{\beta_{1,k} / (c_1 c_k)}. \]

Step 4 of the ring figure runs this chain. No entry fixes the phase of \(c_1\), and step 5 shows that any value of it reproduces the signal, rotated. For a generic signal none of the divisions is by zero, so on a ring of \(n\) points this chain, \(n\) of the \(n(n+1)/2\) distinct entries, determines the signal up to its pose, and every other entry can be recomputed from it. Giannakis (1989) used the same slice to reconstruct signals.

A selective bispectrum is a subset of this kind, enough entries to determine a generic signal up to the group action, with a count that grows linearly with the size of the signal rather than quadratically. For the 256 × 256 image that is 65,536 numbers, one per pixel. Mataigne et al. (2024) select \(O(|G|)\) entries on any finite group by a search over its table of tensor products, like the one for \(D_5\) in the fold-out of part 2, and prove the selection complete for commutative groups, the dihedral groups and the rotations of a cube, with or without reflections. For the 24 rotations of a cube it keeps 172 of the 576 scalars.

The sphere, degree by degree

On the sphere the full bispectrum grows more slowly, as \(L^3\) for \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients, but each entry is a sum of Clebsch–Gordan terms. At \(L = 15\), the setting of the paper's experiments, a signal has 256 coefficients and the full bispectrum 1,124 entries. At \(L = 64\) its 70,257 entries add up 267 million such terms. Our selective version keeps 380 numbers at \(L = 15\) and 5,610 at \(L = 64\), for 25 times less work. On a real signal many of the full entries repeat, and counted once each there are 352 at \(L = 15\) and 22,945 at \(L = 64\). Against those the saving is small at low band-limits and 8-fold at \(L = 64\).

The sphere has no recursion as simple as the ring's, since each degree brings \(2\ell + 1\) unknowns at once, so the construction goes degree by degree. Once the lower degrees are known, an entry \(\beta_{a, b, \ell}\) with \(a, b < \ell\) is linear in \(\mathbf F_\ell\), and the entries kept at degree \(\ell\) form a system in its \(2\ell + 1\) real unknowns. Each degree keeps up to \(2\ell + 1\) such triple products, a few self-couplings \(\beta_{\ell, \ell, \ell'}\), and a few product energies \(\|(\mathbf F_{\ell_1} \otimes \mathbf F_{\ell_2})|_\ell\|^2\), the squared norms of Clebsch–Gordan projections. They are chosen so that the system has full rank, and the total is \(\Theta(L^2)\). The energies are not entries of the bispectrum, so the paper calls the whole descriptor the augmented selective spherical bispectrum. The Earth of part 2 came back from 638 of these numbers.

Testing completeness

The figure below runs the Earth's test on a digit, at \(L\) = 12 like the classifiers and the playground, so that a phone can solve it in a moment. There the descriptor has 254 numbers (196 triple products and 58 product energies) for a signal of 169 coefficients. The paper's, at \(L = 15\), has 380.

What is and isn't proven

For the ring and other commutative groups, for the dihedral groups and the rotations of a cube, and for images on the disk, the selective bispectrum is proven complete for generic signals, by Mataigne et al. (2024) for the groups and by Myers Lantow and Miolane (2026) for the disk. For the sphere we have a count, a local check and an experiment. By the count, a smooth complete invariant needs at least \((L+1)^2 - 3\) numbers, 166 at \(L = 12\), and this one has 254. The check is that at every degree up to \(\ell = 100\), the Jacobian of the kept entries with respect to that degree's coefficients has full rank at random signals, so each degree is locally determined by the lower ones. Being local, this doesn't exclude a distant signal with the same invariants. The signal's mirror image is one such candidate, and the construction includes an entry, \(\beta_{2,3,4}\), that tells it apart. In the experiment, the solver recovered all 32 test signals at \(L = 15\), digits and random band-limited signals, up to a rotation. We conjecture that the selective spherical bispectrum is generically complete, that is complete outside a set of measure zero, but we have no proof.

The strongest experimental result

Spherical MNIST

The classifiers above are small versions of the paper's main experiment, Spherical MNIST (Cohen et al., 2018). Models are trained on upright digits and tested on rotated ones, at \(L = 15\) and over five seeds. The selective bispectrum with an MLP reaches 95.8%. That is not significantly different from a spherical CNN of matched size (96.1%, \(p = 0.14\)), and a quarter of a point below a tensor-product network (\(p = 0.006\)). The planar CNN falls to 13.4%. The power spectrum, invariant but incomplete, reaches 77.7%, and an MLP 48 times larger lifts it only to 80.1%.

It is also fast. A forward pass takes 0.26 ms, 4 times less than the spherical CNN and 364 times less than the tensor-product network. Like the tensor-product network, it does as well on rotated test digits as on upright ones, where the spherical CNN loses about a point.

Two caveats come with this result. The first is data. A fixed invariant with an MLP needs more examples than a spherical CNN that learns its features, and with less training data the spherical CNN leads at every size below the full set, by 13.0 points at 100 examples. The second is the 6 and the 9. Turned upside down, a 6 is a 9, and an invariant model has no way to know. Of the small bispectrum model's 426 mistakes on the 10,000 test digits, 117 are a 6 and a 9 taken for each other. If the label depends on orientation, use a smaller group or keep the pose.

Use cases and the Python library

Three uses

The paper uses the bispectrum in three ways, as a pooling layer, as a set of invariant features and through its inverse.

Pooling. A group convolution applies each filter in every pose of a group, so its feature maps carry an extra axis with one slot per pose. Equivariant networks usually reach an invariant by taking a max over that axis, which keeps one response out of \(|G|\). The bispectrum can take the max's place. The paper does this in two equivariant networks and compares it with up to five alternatives, 46 comparisons in all, and it is never significantly worse. On 3D organ scans under the cube's 24 rotations (OrganMNIST3D), it takes accuracy from 73.6% with max pooling to 78.5% on the same backbone. Its smallest model, with 0.46 million parameters, beats max pooling's largest, with 6.0 million, by 2.6 points. The gain disappears with 250 training examples or fewer, and it is not significant at the largest width. On tissue images under 8 rotations (PatchCamelyon) everything ties, in tests that three seeds per model make sensitive only to gaps of about 0.025 AUC.

Invariant features. With no weights to learn, the bispectrum can also be the whole feature extractor, straight into an MLP. That is the Spherical MNIST model of part 4.

Inversion. Six of the seven modules map their selective bispectrum back to a signal, in a pose of its own, which can then be aligned with the original. On the sphere inversion is still open. SO3onS2.invert raises NotImplementedError, and the solver behind this page's figures lives in the paper's experiment code, not in the library.

The library

bispectrum has one module for each tile of part 2, each a torch.nn.Module without parameters, named {Group}on{Domain}. Calling it returns the selective bispectrum, and selective=False returns the full one, except for DnonDn, SO2onDisk and OctaonOcta, which only have the selective one. For a 32 × 32 image the full one has hundreds of thousands of entries. In the benchmark below, the selective one returns 76 to 1,024 numbers per signal. The modules are differentiable, so they can sit anywhere in a network. In a group-equivariant CNN, one goes where the max pooling was. 

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from bispectrum import CnonCn

# x: output of a group convolution, (batch, channels, 8 poses, height, width)
before = x.max(dim=2).values               # keep the largest of the 8

bsp = CnonCn(n=8)                          # keep everything but the pose
B, C, G, H, W = x.shape
# one ring of 8 per channel and pixel
beta = bsp(x.permute(0, 1, 3, 4, 2).reshape(-1, G))
z = torch.cat([beta.real, beta.imag], dim=-1)
z = z.sign() * z.abs().log1p()
z = z.reshape(B, C, H, W, -1).permute(0, 1, 4, 2, 3).reshape(B, -1, H, W)
after = F.relu(proj(z))                    # proj = nn.Conv2d(16 * C, C, 1)

The ReLU goes after the first learned projection, as here. Placed before it, a ReLU zeroes every negative entry of the bispectrum, which costs 4 to 5 points on OrganMNIST3D.

Two details make the modules fast. When a module is built, it lists every nonzero Clebsch–Gordan term of every kept entry once, so the forward pass is a gather, a product and a scatter-add, with no loop over degrees. With autograd off, it also records its forward pass as a CUDA graph on the first input and replays it for every later input of the same shape, which saves most of the cost of launching small kernels from Python.

Every module in the table takes under a millisecond with autograd on, from 0.07 to 0.67 ms, and 0.03 to 0.20 ms at inference.

Try it

Draw something on the pad, a letter, a face or a continent of your own. It starts with a 0, so clear it first if it's in the way. When you lift the pen, the page computes the 254 numbers of your drawing's selective bispectrum and gives only those to the solver. The reconstruction appears on the right.

Very symmetric drawings, such as a single dot or a centred ring, are the hard case. They are unchanged by every rotation about some axis, so they are far from generic. If the solver gives up, press Reconstruct for other random starts.
