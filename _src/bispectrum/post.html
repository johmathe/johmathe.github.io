NeurIPS 2026

: Selective \(G\)-Bispectra Made Practical

Fast, exact symmetry invariants for PyTorch.

Johan Mathe, Adele Myers Lantow, Simon Mataigne and Nina Miolane · Paper · Code

We trained the three classifiers below on upright digits. For instance, if we rotate the 2, the planar CNN's prediction changes with its orientation. The power-spectrum classifier consistently calls it a 3, while the bispectrum classifier keeps recognizing it as a 2.

The power spectrum tells us how much energy the digit has at each spatial frequency. Two different shapes can have the same energy at every frequency. The bispectrum also measures relationships between frequencies, and those relationships can tell the shapes apart. We built bispectrum, a PyTorch library that computes these features for seven group actions.

Invariance in classifiers

The top model in the figure is a small CNN that reads the sphere as a 64 × 128 image. It is right on 99.1% of upright test digits and on 13.3% of rotated ones, close to chance, and it reads the 2 on its side as an 8. Nothing in its weights says that a turned 2 is still a 2. There are three standard ways to put that in.

Data augmentation. The network trains on rotated copies of every digit. With online augmentation, each step draws new poses, so the number of training steps can stay the same. The learned invariance is approximate, and it holds for the poses and digits the network was shown.

Max pooling over poses. A group convolution applies each filter in every pose of a group \(G\), which adds an axis with one slot per pose. Transforming the input permutes that axis and can also move the feature maps in space. A max over the axis removes the dependence on the permutation, but the spatial coordinates can still transform, so a fully invariant model also pools over space. The max keeps one response out of \(|G|\) and can map two different inputs to the same value.

Fourier magnitudes. A shift multiplies each Fourier coefficient \(c_k\) by a phase and leaves \(|c_k|\) alone, so the power spectrum \(|c_k|^2\) is invariant for free. On the sphere it is one energy per degree, or 13 numbers for these digits. An MLP on them, the middle model, gives the same answer in every pose, and for this 2 the answer is a 3 (70%). It scores 77.6% upright and 77.4% rotated. The invariance is exact and the model still fails, because magnitudes record how much of the digit sits at each scale and nothing about how the pieces line up.

The bottom model uses the bispectrum, which is exactly invariant and also keeps the relations between frequencies that the power spectrum drops. The rest of this article builds it on a ring, then on a group, then on the sphere.

The bispectrum and what it preserves

Invariant and complete

We write \(g \cdot x\) for a signal \(x\) transformed by an element \(g\) of a group \(G\). We call \(g\) the signal's pose, and the set of all its transformed copies its orbit. A map \(\beta\) is invariant if it is constant on orbits, and complete if it also tells orbits apart,

\[ \beta(x) = \beta(y) \iff y = g \cdot x \text{ for some } g \in G. \]

An invariant has to forget the pose, and a complete one forgets nothing else. Following the paper, we call \(\beta(x)\) the signal's invariant descriptor.

Counting dimensions gives a floor on the size of a complete invariant that varies smoothly with the signal. The digits in the figure are kept as 169 coefficients. A digit with no rotational symmetry has a three-dimensional orbit, so the orbits form a space of 166 dimensions, and a smooth complete invariant needs at least that many numbers. The count is necessary and not sufficient. Max pooling over 8 rotations keeps 1 response out of 8, and the power spectrum keeps 13 numbers, so neither can be complete.

Recovering one Fourier coefficient at a time

The ring shows the problem and the fix in their simplest form. A periodic function \(r(\theta)\), drawn as a closed curve of radius \(r(\theta)\) at angle \(\theta\), is a mean radius \(c_0\) plus 8 harmonics,

\[ r(\theta) = c_0 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{8} |c_k| \cos(k\theta + \varphi_k). \]

The \(c_k = |c_k|\, e^{i\varphi_k}\) are the Fourier coefficients of \(r\), with the sign convention of torch.fft. Turning the curve by \(\alpha\) replaces \(r(\theta)\) with \(r(\theta - \alpha)\) and multiplies each coefficient by a phase,

\[ c_k \mapsto e^{-ik\alpha}\, c_k. \]

A rotation moves \((\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_8)\) along a line, \(\varphi_k\) falling \(k\) times as fast as \(\varphi_1\). The power spectrum drops the phases altogether, so it can't tell a rotation from a scramble that draws new phases at random. Phases add under multiplication, so a product of coefficients whose frequencies sum to zero is invariant. The smallest such products that still see the phases are triple products,

\[ \beta_{k_1, k_2} = c_{k_1}\, c_{k_2}\, \overline{c_{k_1 + k_2}} \;\mapsto\; e^{-i(k_1 + k_2 - (k_1 + k_2))\alpha}\, \beta_{k_1, k_2} = \beta_{k_1, k_2}. \]

The phase of \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is \(\varphi_{k_1} + \varphi_{k_2} - \varphi_{k_1 + k_2}\), a relation between three phases that a rotation preserves and a scramble breaks. The table of all \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is the bispectrum, named for its two frequency indices. It is the Fourier transform, over two lags, of the triple correlation \(\int r(\theta)\, r(\theta + s_1)\, r(\theta + s_2)\, d\theta\), and on the ring each entry is a triple product (Tukey, 1953). Its row \(k_1 = 0\) is \(c_0 |c_k|^2\), so it gives the power spectrum whenever \(c_0 \neq 0\).

A few entries are enough to rebuild the curve. The first two, \(\beta_{0,0} = c_0^3\) and \(\beta_{0,1} = c_0 |c_1|^2\), give us the mean radius and the magnitude of the first harmonic. On the continuous circle, choosing the phase of that harmonic sets the orientation of the curve. Once we know \(c_1\), the entry \(\beta_{1,1} = c_1^2\, \overline{c_2}\) gives us \(c_2\). We can then use \(\beta_{1,2} = c_1 c_2\, \overline{c_3}\) to recover \(c_3\). The same calculation takes us through \(c_8\),

\[ c_{k+1} = \overline{\beta_{1,k} / (c_1 c_k)}, \]

and each step divides by \(c_1 c_k\), so the recovery needs \(c_0\) and every \(c_k\) to be nonzero. A subset of entries like this one, enough to determine the signal up to its pose and small next to the full table, is a selective bispectrum.

On a ring of \(n\) samples, the cyclic group \(C_n\), the same algebra holds with frequencies taken modulo \(n\), and the recovery runs to \(c_{n-1}\). Here only the \(n\) shifts by \(2\pi m / n\) are symmetries, so not every choice of \(\arg c_1\) gives a sampled real signal. Setting \(\arg c_1 = 0\) returns \(c_k e^{-ik\phi}\), where \(\phi = \arg c_1\) of the input, which is a turn by a continuous angle. For a real signal \(c_{n-1} = \overline{c_1}\), so the recovered \(c_{n-1}\) has phase \(-n\phi\), known modulo \(2\pi\). Multiplying every recovered \(c_k\) by \(e^{ik\theta}\), with \(\theta = -\arg(c_{n-1}) / n\), moves the result to a multiple of \(2\pi / n\), and the inverse DFT is then a real cyclic shift of the input. This final phase adjustment is Algorithm 2 in Appendix C of Mataigne et al. (2024). CnonCn.invert in release 0.5.0 skips it and returns a complex signal, with an imaginary part of about 3.15 on the input \([1, 2, 4, 8]\). A fix for the next release applies it, in CnonCn and TorusOnTorus, and tests the result against torch.roll of the input.

Generalizing the bispectrum to more symmetries

The Fourier transform on a group

The triple product works because the Fourier basis turns a rotation into one scalar phase per coefficient. Other symmetries need the Fourier transform of their own group. A signal on a group, such as the output of a group convolution, has one value \(f(g)\) per pose \(g \in G\) for each channel and pixel. Its Fourier coefficients are indexed by the group's irreducible representations, or irreps. An irrep is a map \(\rho\) from \(G\) to \(d_\rho \times d_\rho\) unitary matrices that respects composition, \(\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\, \rho(h)\), and that no change of basis splits into smaller blocks. The coefficient at \(\rho\) is the matrix

\[ \hat f(\rho) = \sum_{g \in G} f(g)\, \rho(g)^\dagger \;\in\; \mathbb{C}^{d_\rho \times d_\rho}. \]

Irreps play the role of frequencies. On the cyclic group \(C_n\) they are all \(1 \times 1\), \(\rho_k(g) = e^{2\pi i k g / n}\) for \(k = 0, \dots, n - 1\), and \(\hat f(\rho_k)\) is the \(k\)-th coefficient of the discrete Fourier transform, in the convention used above. When the group doesn't commute, as with the rotations of a cube or the turns and flips of a polygon, some irreps are matrices, and so are the coefficients.

Two properties of the DFT carry over. The transform is invertible, and since \(\sum_\rho d_\rho^2 = |G|\), it holds exactly as many numbers as the signal. And a shift becomes a multiplication. If \((h \cdot f)(g) = f(h^{-1} g)\), then

\[ \widehat{h \cdot f}(\rho) = \hat f(\rho)\, \rho(h)^\dagger, \]

the matrix version of the phase \(e^{-ik\alpha}\) on the ring. For continuous groups such as SO(2) and SO(3), the sum becomes an integral and there are infinitely many irreps, so an implementation keeps those up to a cutoff. For a signal on a space that the group acts on, such as the sphere, the coefficients are vectors rather than square matrices.

The \(G\)-bispectrum

On the ring, the phases cancel because frequencies add. The product \(c_{k_1} c_{k_2}\) turns by \(e^{-i(k_1 + k_2)\alpha}\), exactly like \(c_{k_1 + k_2}\). With matrix coefficients, the phases become matrices that don't commute, and the product of two coefficients is a tensor product \(\hat f(\rho_1) \otimes \hat f(\rho_2)\), which a shift multiplies on the right by \((\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(h)^\dagger\). The representation \(\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2\) is usually not irreducible, but a fixed unitary change of basis \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\), the matrix of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, splits it into irreps,

\[ C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}^\dagger\, (\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(g)\, C_{\rho_1 \rho_2} = \bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \rho(g), \]

where an irrep that occurs several times in \(\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2\) appears that many times in the sum. This decomposition takes the place of "frequencies add". The \(G\)-bispectrum pairs the tensor product with the conjugate coefficients at the irreps it contains. Writing \(C\) for \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\),

\[ \beta_{\rho_1, \rho_2} = \big[\hat f(\rho_1) \otimes \hat f(\rho_2)\big]\, C \Big[\bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \hat f(\rho)^\dagger\Big]\, C^\dagger. \]

Under a shift by \(h\), the first factor picks up \(C \big[\bigoplus \rho(h)^\dagger\big] C^\dagger\) on the right, the block-diagonal factor picks up \(\bigoplus \rho(h)\) on its left, and the two cancel, as the phases did on the ring. There every irrep is \(1 \times 1\), \(C = 1\), and the formula is the ring's triple product again. As on the ring, \(\beta\) is the Fourier transform, now on \(G \times G\), of the \(G\)-triple correlation \(T(g_1, g_2) = \sum_{g} f(g)\, f(g g_1)\, f(g g_2)\) (Kakarala, 1992). Each block \(\beta_{\rho_1, \rho_2}\) is a \(d_{\rho_1} d_{\rho_2} \times d_{\rho_1} d_{\rho_2}\) matrix, so one block holds many scalar entries. The same recipe, with each group's irreps and Clebsch–Gordan matrices, gives every module of the library.

The \(G\)-bispectrum is invariant for every one of these groups. Kakarala (1992) proved that on a finite group it is also complete, up to the group action, when every Fourier coefficient \(\hat f(\rho)\) is an invertible matrix. We call such signals generic. The exceptions form a set of measure zero, so a signal drawn from any distribution with a density is generic with probability one. On the ring the condition is that no \(c_k\) is zero.

The smallest groups with irreps larger than \(1 \times 1\) are the dihedral groups \(D_n\), the \(n\) turns and \(n\) flips of a regular \(n\)-gon. They are generated by a turn \(a\) and a flip \(x\) with \(x a x = a^{-1}\), so turning and then flipping is not the same as flipping and then turning. Two irreps of \(D_n\) are \(1 \times 1\) (four when \(n\) is even) and send each element to \(\pm 1\). The rest are \(2 \times 2\),

\[ \rho_k(a) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} & -\sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} \\ \sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} & \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_k(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad 0 < k < n/2. \]

These two matrices don't commute, so no change of basis diagonalizes both, and a shift mixes the columns of each \(2 \times 2\) coefficient. The tensor product \(\rho_i \otimes \rho_j\) of two \(2 \times 2\) irreps splits into \(\rho_{i+j} \oplus \rho_{i-j}\). Indices are read modulo \(n\) and up to sign, and a block whose index is a multiple of \(n/2\) splits further into two \(1 \times 1\) irreps. The Kronecker table of \(D_5\) collects these splits.

On the ring each cell would hold the single frequency \(k_1 + k_2\). The Clebsch–Gordan matrix \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\) is the change of basis that performs each split, and the \(G\)-bispectrum has one block per cell of the table. The block of \(E_1 \otimes E_1\) is a \(4 \times 4\) matrix, 16 scalar entries.

The sphere

On the sphere, under SO(3), the irreps are indexed by a degree \(\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and the matching basis functions are the spherical harmonics \(Y_\ell^m\), \(-\ell \le m \le \ell\). The coefficients of degree \(\ell\) form a vector \(\mathbf F_\ell = (a_\ell^{-\ell}, \dots, a_\ell^{\ell}) \in \mathbb C^{2\ell + 1}\), and a rotation \(R\) acts on it by a unitary matrix of size \(2\ell + 1\), the Wigner D-matrix \(D^\ell(R)\),

\[ \mathbf F_\ell \mapsto D^\ell(R)\, \mathbf F_\ell. \]

Degrees never mix, but the coefficients within a degree do. In practice a signal is kept up to some degree \(L\), its band-limit, which leaves \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients. The power spectrum is \(\|\mathbf F_\ell\|^2\), one number per degree. The spherical bispectrum projects the product of two degrees onto a third with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and pairs it with that degree's coefficients,

\[ \beta_{\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell} = \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} \; \sum_{m_1 + m_2 = m} C^{\ell m}_{\ell_1 m_1 \ell_2 m_2} \; a_{\ell_1}^{m_1}\, a_{\ell_2}^{m_2}\, \overline{a_\ell^{m}}, \]

one number for each triple of degrees with \(|\ell_1 - \ell_2| \le \ell \le \ell_1 + \ell_2\). The projected product and \(\mathbf F_\ell\) transform by the same unitary \(D^\ell(R)\), so their inner product is invariant. The squared norm of the projected product, \(\|(\mathbf F_{\ell_1} \otimes \mathbf F_{\ell_2})|_\ell\|^2\), is invariant too. It is quartic in the coefficients, and the paper calls it a Clebsch–Gordan power coefficient.

Kakarala's theorem does not cover this case. The sphere is a space that SO(3) acts on, not the group itself, so its coefficients are vectors, and a theorem about invertible matrices doesn't apply (Kondor, 2007).

The bottom model of the figure at the top reads the augmented selective spherical bispectrum of the digit, a subset of the cubic entries chosen degree by degree plus a few Clebsch–Gordan power coefficients. Its construction comes below. At \(L = 12\) it has 254 invariant values, 196 cubic and 58 quartic. In the paper's experiment, at \(L = 15\), it has 303 cubic entries and 77 quartic ones, or 380 invariant values, stored as 380 complex slots that become 760 real channels for the MLP.

Rebuilding the Earth

Invariance is easy to check by rotating the signal. Completeness, the claim that nothing but the pose is lost, can be tested by rebuilding a signal from its invariants alone. The figure takes the Earth's land mask in spherical harmonics up to degree 20, or 441 coefficients. Its power spectrum is 21 numbers, and random globes with the same 21 look nothing like the Earth. Its augmented selective spherical bispectrum has 638 values, sums of products of three or four coefficients. From those alone, a numerical solver rebuilds the land mask in a pose of its own.

The solver runs from several random starts. It chooses among the candidates by how closely their invariants match the target's, with ties broken by a fixed order that does not depend on the target, and it never compares a candidate with the original. Only after the choice is the original used, to rotate the chosen globe onto it and to measure the error. Rotated onto the original, the rebuilt band-limited harmonic coefficients match the original's to a relative error of about \(10^{-12}\), or 12 digits.

How selective bispectra reduce complexity

The cost of the full bispectrum

The full bispectrum has an entry for every pair of irreps, so on a finite group it holds \(O(|G|^2)\) scalars, quadratic in the size of the signal. A 32 × 32 image under cyclic shifts has 1,024 pixels and a full bispectrum of 524,800 entries. At 256 × 256 the FFT gives 65,536 coefficients and the bispectrum 2,147,516,416 entries, about 17 GB at 8 bytes each, per image and per forward pass.

Selective \(G\)-bispectra on finite groups

Most of those entries are redundant. On a ring of \(n\) points, the \(n\) entries of the recovery above determine a signal with nonzero coefficients up to a shift (Giannakis, 1989), and every other entry of the \(n(n+1)/2\) can be recomputed from them. For the 256 × 256 image that is 65,536 numbers, half a megabyte.

On a finite group, Mataigne et al. (2024) select \(O(|G|)\) scalar entries by a breadth-first search over the table of tensor products, like the \(D_5\) table in the fold-out, and call the result the selective \(G\)-bispectrum. For the 24 rotations of a cube they keep 172 of the 576 scalars. The search runs on any finite group. Their completeness theorems cover the commutative groups, the dihedral groups and the octahedral groups, for signals whose Fourier coefficients are all nonsingular.

The sphere, degree by degree

On the sphere the full bispectrum grows more slowly, as \(L^3\) entries for \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients, but each entry is a sum of Clebsch–Gordan terms. At \(L = 15\), the setting of the paper's experiments, a signal has 256 coefficients and the full bispectrum 1,124 stored entries. The augmented selective spherical bispectrum keeps 380 values at \(L = 15\) and 5,610 at \(L = 64\).

At \(L = 64\), three ratios measure three different things. The full bispectrum stores 70,257 entries, which is 12.52 times the selective count. On a real signal, orderings of a triple of degrees are proportional and some entries vanish, so only 22,945 distinct nonzero entries remain, 4.09 times the selective count. At \(L = 15\) there are 352, fewer than the selective 380. In contraction terms, the full bispectrum sums 267,050,641 products and the selective one 10,837,962, a ratio of 24.64. Asymptotically the selective output has \(\Theta(L^2)\) entries and costs \(O(L^4)\) terms, against \(O(L^5)\) for the full bispectrum. Output size, arithmetic and measured latency are different quantities, and the latencies are below.

The sphere has no recovery as simple as the ring's, since each degree brings \(2\ell + 1\) unknowns at once, so the construction goes degree by degree. Once the lower degrees are known, an entry \(\beta_{a, b, \ell}\) with \(a, b < \ell\) is linear in \(\overline{\mathbf F_\ell}\). Each degree keeps up to \(2\ell + 1\) of these, plus a few self-couplings \(\beta_{\ell, \ell, \ell'}\) and Clebsch–Gordan power coefficients, chosen so that the system for \(\mathbf F_\ell\) has full rank. The total is \(\Theta(L^2)\).

Completeness of selective \(G\)-bispectra is proven for the group families above (Mataigne et al., 2024). On the disk, the coefficients \(a_{n,k}\) of all radial orders \(k\) at angular order \(n\) get the same phase \(e^{in\phi}\) under a rotation by \(\phi\), and the selective disk bispectrum of Myers Lantow and Miolane (2026) is complete for images with \(a_{n,1} \neq 0\) for every retained angular order. For a single real band-limited signal on the sphere, generic global completeness is a conjecture. The count meets the necessary bound, with 254 values at \(L = 12\) against 166. At random signals, the Jacobian of each degree's entries with respect to that degree's coefficients has full rank up to \(\ell = 100\), which shows local recovery of each degree with the lower degrees fixed. The odd-parity entry \(\beta_{2,3,4}\) changes sign under a mirror, so it detects a mirror image whenever it is nonzero. And the solver recovered all 32 test signals at \(L = 15\) up to a rotation, which is empirical evidence. None of these rules out a distant signal with the same invariants.

Classifying digits at unseen rotations

Spherical MNIST

The classifiers at the top are small versions of the paper's main experiment, Spherical MNIST (Cohen et al., 2018), with digits on a 64 × 128 spherical grid, training on upright digits, testing on rotated ones, and five seeds. Our model takes the augmented selective spherical bispectrum at \(L = 15\), applies \(\mathrm{sign}(x) \log(1 + |x|)\) to the real and imaginary parts, and feeds the 760 channels to a three-layer MLP. We compare it with a spherical CNN and an e3nn tensor-product network matched in size, and with two controls, the power spectrum with an MLP and a planar CNN.

The selective bispectrum with an MLP reaches 95.8% on rotated digits, and both equivariant networks reach 96.1%, 0.3 points higher. Over five seeds the spherical CNN's lead has \(p = 0.14\), which does not show that the two models are equivalent, and the tensor-product network's has \(p = 0.006\). Like the tensor-product network, our model scores the same on rotated test digits as on upright ones, while the spherical CNN drops from 97.1% to 96.1%. The planar CNN falls to 13.4%. The power spectrum is just as invariant and reaches 77.7%, and an MLP 48 times larger lifts it only to 80.1%, so the gap comes from what the features keep, not from the size of the head.

The full training set favors our model more than small ones do. With all of it the spherical CNN leads by 0.3 points, and it leads at every smaller training-set size, by 13.0 points at 100 examples and by 1.3 points at 12,500. The spherical CNN learns its features, and our model's are fixed. Our model still beats the tensor-product network up to 2,500 examples.

The classifiers in the figure at the top differ from the paper's experiment in three ways, and their accuracy is close to the paper's.

An invariant model can't tell a 6 from an upside-down 9. The browser model makes 426 mistakes on the 10,000 upright test digits and 428 on the rotated ones, and 117 of the first and 117 of the second are a 6 and a 9 taken for each other. When the label depends on orientation, a smaller group or the pose as an extra feature keeps that information.

Latency

On an H100, in float32 at batch 16, the median forward pass from the spherical grid to the logits takes 0.257 ms for our model, 1.02 ms for the spherical CNN and 93.7 ms for the tensor-product network, which is 4 and 364 times our model's time. The architectures explain most of it. Both equivariant networks do their group theory in every layer, the spherical CNN with learned convolutions over rotations and the tensor-product network with Clebsch–Gordan products of learned features. Our model does it once, in a fixed layer without weights, and passes 760 real numbers to a plain MLP.

Three implementation choices keep that layer cheap. Selectivity makes the number of entries, and the work to compute them, grow like the signal rather than its square. Precomputation moves the group theory to construction time. Every module stores its Clebsch–Gordan matrices and Fourier kernels as buffers, and on the sphere it also lists every nonzero Clebsch–Gordan term of every kept entry once, cached on disk, so the forward pass is a gather, a product and a scatter-add over one flat table. And at batch 16 a forward pass is a few dozen small kernels, whose wall-clock is set by the host launching them more than by the GPU. With autograd off, each module records its forward pass as a CUDA graph on the first input and replays it as one launch for every later input of the same shape.

In these six configurations, each selective module takes 0.07 to 0.67 ms forward with autograd and 0.03 to 0.20 ms at inference, and returns 76 to 1,024 numbers per signal. Other sizes, batch sizes and GPUs were not timed here.

Using the library

bispectrum installs with pip install bispectrum on Python 3.12 and depends on PyTorch, NumPy and torch-harmonics. There is one module per tile of the group figure, each a torch.nn.Module without parameters, named {Group}on{Domain}. Calling a module returns its selective bispectrum. The modules are differentiable, so they can sit anywhere in a network. For the groups where it exists, selective=False returns the full bispectrum instead. DnonDn, SO2onDisk and OctaonOcta only have the selective one.

Six of the seven modules have an invert method that maps a selective bispectrum back to a signal in a pose of its own, which can then be aligned with the original. On the sphere, SO3onS2.invert raises NotImplementedError, and the solver behind this page's figures lives in the paper's experiment code, not in the library.

Pooling in an equivariant network

In a group-equivariant CNN with 8 rotations, each channel and pixel carries a ring of 8 pose responses. CnonCn replaces the max over that ring. Each ring comes back as 8 complex invariants where the max kept 1 real number. As in the paper, the invariants are split into real and imaginary parts, compressed with a signed log, and mixed back down to \(C\) channels with a 1 × 1 convolution. 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from bispectrum import CnonCn

B, C, G, H, W = 4, 16, 8, 28, 28
x = torch.randn(B, C, G, H, W)  # group conv output, 8 poses per channel and pixel
proj = nn.Conv2d(2 * G * C, C, kernel_size=1)

before = x.max(dim=2).values  # (B, C, H, W), the largest of the 8

bsp = CnonCn(n=G)
rings = x.permute(0, 1, 3, 4, 2).reshape(-1, G)  # (B·C·H·W, 8)
beta = bsp(rings)  # (B·C·H·W, 8), complex
z = torch.cat([beta.real, beta.imag], dim=-1)  # (B·C·H·W, 16)
z = z.sign() * z.abs().log1p()  # signed log
z = z.reshape(B, C, H, W, 2 * G).permute(0, 1, 4, 2, 3).reshape(B, 2 * G * C, H, W)
after = F.relu(proj(z))  # (B, C, H, W)

Like the max, this removes the dependence on a cyclic shift of the pose axis, and the feature maps can still move in space. It also treats each channel's ring separately, so it is invariant to a different shift in every channel, and it discards the relative orientation between channels. In the paper's networks the ReLU comes after the first learned projection, as here. Placed before it, a ReLU zeroes every negative invariant, which cost 4 to 5 points on OrganMNIST3D.

Across two networks and up to five alternatives, 46 comparisons in all, bispectral pooling is never significantly worse. On 3D organ scans under the cube's 24 rotations (OrganMNIST3D), it lifts accuracy from 73.6% with max pooling to 78.5% on the same backbone, and its smallest model (0.46M parameters) beats max pooling's largest (6.0M) by 2.6 points. The gain disappears with 250 training examples or fewer and is not significant at the largest width. On tissue images under 8 rotations (PatchCamelyon) we observed no significant differences. With three seeds per model, those tests had 80% power for a gap of about 0.025 AUC, so smaller gaps can't be ruled out.

In the browser

The figures on this page run the same math in TypeScript, at \(L = 12\). The pad below starts with a 0, and “clear” empties it. When the pen lifts, the page computes the 254 invariants of the drawing and gives only those to the solver, which picks its candidate by invariant residual alone. The drawing is used afterwards, to align the result and measure its error. When several distinct signals match, the candidate button steps through them, and a run that misses the tolerance is reported as a miss. Very symmetric drawings, such as a dot or a centered ring, are unchanged by every rotation about some axis and so far from generic, and the solver often fails on them.

Conclusion

The bispectrum is an exact invariant, complete for generic signals on the groups covered by Kakarala's theorem, and its full form is quadratic in the size of the signal. Selective bispectra keep a number of entries linear in that size, and bispectrum computes them as one PyTorch module per group action. Issues and pull requests are welcome.

The largest open question is on the sphere. For a single real band-limited signal, does the augmented selective spherical bispectrum determine almost every signal up to rotation? The count, the per-degree Jacobians, the parity entry and the 32 reconstructions are all consistent with it, and none of them is a proof.
