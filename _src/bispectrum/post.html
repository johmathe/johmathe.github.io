NeurIPS 2026

: Selective \(G\)-Bispectra Made Practical

Fast, exact, lossless symmetry invariants for PyTorch.

Johan Mathe, Adele Myers Lantow, Simon Mataigne and Nina Miolane · Paper · Code

We built bispectrum, a PyTorch library that turns signals into features with built-in invariance to a chosen symmetry. It uses the \(G\)-bispectrum to capture relationships across frequencies that survive transformations such as rotations or translations. A selective algorithm and GPU optimizations keep feature extraction under a millisecond per batch on a GPU, and on Spherical MNIST our model runs 4 times faster than a spherical CNN and 364 times faster than an e3nn tensor-product network.

Invariance in classifiers

The top model in the figure is a small CNN that reads the sphere as a 64 × 128 image. It is right on 99.1% of upright test digits and on 13.3% of rotated ones, close to chance, and it reads the 2 on its side as an 8. Nothing in its weights says that a turned 2 is still a 2. There are three standard ways to put that in.

Data augmentation. Train on rotated copies of every digit. Training cost grows with the number of poses, and the learned invariance is approximate, good for the poses and digits the network was shown.

Max pooling over poses. A group convolution applies each filter in every pose of a group \(G\), which adds an axis with one slot per pose. A max over that axis is invariant by construction, keeps one response out of \(|G|\), and can map two different inputs to the same value.

Fourier magnitudes. A shift multiplies each Fourier coefficient \(c_k\) by a phase and leaves \(|c_k|\) alone, so the power spectrum \(|c_k|^2\) is invariant for free. On the sphere it is one energy per degree, or 13 numbers for these digits. An MLP on them, the middle model, gives the same answer in every pose, and for this 2 the answer is a 3 (70%). It scores 77.6% upright and 77.4% rotated. The invariance is exact and the model still fails, because magnitudes say how much of the digit sits at each scale and nothing about how the pieces line up.

The bottom model uses the bispectrum, which keeps the exact invariance of the last two without their losses. The rest of this post builds it on a ring, then on any group, then on the sphere.

The bispectrum and what it preserves

Invariant and complete

Let's write \(g \cdot x\) for a signal \(x\) transformed by an element \(g\) of a group \(G\). We call \(g\) the signal's pose, and the set of all its transformed copies its orbit. A map \(\Phi\) is invariant if it is constant on orbits, and complete if it also tells orbits apart,

\[ \Phi(x) = \Phi(y) \iff y = g \cdot x \text{ for some } g \in G. \]

An invariant has to forget the pose, and a complete one forgets nothing else. Following the paper, we call \(\Phi(x)\) the signal's invariant descriptor.

Counting dimensions gives a floor on the size of a complete invariant that varies smoothly with the signal. The digits in the figure are kept as 169 coefficients. A digit with no rotational symmetry has a three-dimensional orbit, so the orbits form a space of 166 dimensions, and a smooth complete invariant needs at least that many numbers. Max pooling over 8 rotations keeps 1 response out of 8, and the power spectrum keeps 13 numbers. Neither can be complete.

Triple products on a ring

The ring shows the problem and the fix in their simplest form. Take a periodic function \(r(\theta)\), drawn as a closed curve of radius \(r(\theta)\) at angle \(\theta\), and write it as a mean radius \(c_0\) plus 8 waves,

\[ r(\theta) = c_0 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^{8} |c_k| \cos(k\theta - \varphi_k). \]

The \(c_k = |c_k|\, e^{i\varphi_k}\) are the Fourier coefficients of \(r\), conjugated so that turning the curve by \(\alpha\) advances the phases,

\[ c_k \mapsto e^{ik\alpha}\, c_k. \]

A rotation moves \((\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_8)\) along a line, \(\varphi_k\) advancing \(k\) times as fast as \(\varphi_1\). The power spectrum drops the phases altogether, so it can't tell a rotation from a scramble that draws new phases at random (step 2 of the figure below). Phases add under multiplication, so a product of coefficients whose frequencies sum to zero is invariant. The smallest such products that still see the phases are triple products,

\[ \beta_{k_1, k_2} = c_{k_1}\, c_{k_2}\, \overline{c_{k_1 + k_2}} \;\mapsto\; e^{i(k_1 + k_2 - (k_1 + k_2))\alpha}\, \beta_{k_1, k_2} = \beta_{k_1, k_2}. \]

The phase of \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is \(\varphi_{k_1} + \varphi_{k_2} - \varphi_{k_1 + k_2}\), a relation between three phases that a rotation preserves and a scramble breaks. The table of all \(\beta_{k_1, k_2}\) is the bispectrum, named for its two frequency indices. Its row \(k_1 = 0\) is \(c_0 |c_k|^2\), so it contains the power spectrum. On a ring of \(n\) samples, the cyclic group \(C_n\), the same algebra holds with \(\alpha\) a multiple of \(2\pi/n\) and frequencies taken modulo \(n\), which is the bispectrum of classical signal processing (Tukey, 1953). The figure plots the entries with \(k_1 \le k_2\) and \(k_1 + k_2 \le 8\) by phase, next to the plane of \((\varphi_1, \varphi_2)\), where a rotation moves the signal's point along a line of slope 2.

The library's logo is this triangle of three coefficients whose phases cancel under rotation, set in the round counter of a b.

Generalizing the bispectrum to more symmetries

The Fourier transform on a group

The triple product works because the Fourier basis turns a rotation into one phase per coefficient. Other symmetries need the Fourier transform of their own group. Take a signal on a group, such as the output of a group convolution, with one value \(f(g)\) per pose \(g \in G\) for each channel and pixel. Its Fourier coefficients are indexed by the group's irreducible representations, or irreps. An irrep is a map \(\rho\) from \(G\) to \(d_\rho \times d_\rho\) unitary matrices that respects composition, \(\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\, \rho(h)\), and that no change of basis splits into smaller blocks. The coefficient at \(\rho\) is the matrix

\[ \hat f(\rho) = \sum_{g \in G} f(g)\, \rho(g)^\dagger \;\in\; \mathbb{C}^{d_\rho \times d_\rho}. \]

Irreps play the role of frequencies. On the cyclic group \(C_n\) they are all \(1 \times 1\), \(\rho_k(g) = e^{2\pi i k g / n}\) for \(k = 0, \dots, n - 1\), and \(\hat f(\rho_k)\) is the \(k\)-th coefficient of the discrete Fourier transform. When the group doesn't commute, as with the rotations of a cube or the turns and flips of a polygon, some irreps are matrices, and so are the coefficients.

Two properties of the DFT carry over. The transform is invertible, and since \(\sum_\rho d_\rho^2 = |G|\), it holds exactly as many numbers as the signal. And a shift becomes a multiplication. If \((h \cdot f)(g) = f(h^{-1} g)\), then

\[ \widehat{h \cdot f}(\rho) = \hat f(\rho)\, \rho(h)^\dagger, \]

the matrix version of the phase that a shift puts on each Fourier coefficient. For continuous groups such as SO(2) and SO(3), the sum becomes an integral and there are infinitely many irreps, so an implementation keeps those up to a cutoff. For a signal on a space that the group acts on, such as the sphere, the coefficients are vectors rather than square matrices.

The \(G\)-bispectrum

On the ring, the phase of \(c_{k_1} c_{k_2}\) turns like the phase of \(c_{k_1 + k_2}\). With matrices, the product of two coefficients is a tensor product \(\hat f(\rho_1) \otimes \hat f(\rho_2)\), which a shift multiplies on the right by \((\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(h)^\dagger\). The representation \(\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2\) is usually not irreducible, but a fixed unitary change of basis \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\), the matrix of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, splits it into irreps,

\[ C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}^\dagger\, (\rho_1 \otimes \rho_2)(g)\, C_{\rho_1 \rho_2} = \bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \rho(g). \]

The \(G\)-bispectrum pairs the tensor product with the conjugate coefficients at the irreps it contains. Writing \(C\) for \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\),

\[ \beta_{\rho_1, \rho_2} = \big[\hat f(\rho_1) \otimes \hat f(\rho_2)\big]\, C \Big[\bigoplus_{\rho \in \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2} \hat f(\rho)^\dagger\Big]\, C^\dagger. \]

Under a shift by \(h\), the first factor picks up \(C \big[\bigoplus \rho(h)^\dagger\big] C^\dagger\) on the right, the block-diagonal factor picks up \(\bigoplus \rho(h)\) on its left, and the two cancel, as the phases did on the ring. There every irrep is \(1 \times 1\), \(C = 1\), and the formula is the ring's triple product again, up to conjugation. The same recipe, with each group's irreps and Clebsch–Gordan matrices, gives every module of the library.

The \(G\)-bispectrum is invariant for every one of these groups. Kakarala (1992) proved that on a finite group it is also complete, up to the group action, when every Fourier coefficient \(\hat f(\rho)\) is an invertible matrix. We call such signals generic. The exceptions form a set of measure zero, so a random signal is generic with probability one. On the ring the condition is that no \(c_k\) is zero.

The smallest groups with irreps larger than \(1 \times 1\) are the dihedral groups \(D_n\), the \(n\) turns and \(n\) flips of a regular \(n\)-gon. They are generated by a turn \(a\) and a flip \(x\) with \(x a x = a^{-1}\), so turning and then flipping is not the same as flipping and then turning. Two irreps of \(D_n\) are \(1 \times 1\) (four when \(n\) is even) and send each element to \(\pm 1\). The rest are \(2 \times 2\),

\[ \rho_k(a) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} & -\sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} \\ \sin \frac{2\pi k}{n} & \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_k(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad 0 < k < n/2. \]

These two matrices don't commute, so no change of basis diagonalizes both, and a shift mixes the columns of each \(2 \times 2\) coefficient. The tensor product \(\rho_i \otimes \rho_j\) of two \(2 \times 2\) irreps splits into \(\rho_{i+j} \oplus \rho_{i-j}\). Indices are read modulo \(n\) and up to sign, and a block whose index is a multiple of \(n/2\) splits further into two \(1 \times 1\) irreps. For \(D_5\) the splits are these.

On the ring each cell would hold the single frequency \(k_1 + k_2\). The Clebsch–Gordan matrix \(C_{\rho_1 \rho_2}\) is the change of basis that performs each split, and the \(G\)-bispectrum has one entry per cell of the table.

The sphere

On the sphere, under SO(3), the irreps are indexed by a degree \(\ell = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), and the matching basis functions are the spherical harmonics \(Y_\ell^m\), \(-\ell \le m \le \ell\). The coefficients of degree \(\ell\) form a vector \(\mathbf F_\ell = (a_\ell^{-\ell}, \dots, a_\ell^{\ell}) \in \mathbb C^{2\ell + 1}\), and a rotation \(R\) acts on it by a unitary matrix of size \(2\ell + 1\), the Wigner D-matrix \(D^\ell(R)\),

\[ \mathbf F_\ell \mapsto D^\ell(R)\, \mathbf F_\ell. \]

Degrees never mix, but the coefficients within a degree do. In practice a signal is kept up to some degree \(L\), its band-limit, which leaves \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients. The power spectrum is \(\|\mathbf F_\ell\|^2\), one number per degree. The spherical bispectrum projects the product of two degrees onto a third with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and pairs it with that degree's coefficients,

\[ \beta_{\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell} = \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} \; \sum_{m_1 + m_2 = m} C^{\ell m}_{\ell_1 m_1 \ell_2 m_2} \; a_{\ell_1}^{m_1}\, a_{\ell_2}^{m_2}\, \overline{a_\ell^{m}}, \]

one number for each triple of degrees with \(|\ell_1 - \ell_2| \le \ell \le \ell_1 + \ell_2\). The projected product and \(\mathbf F_\ell\) transform by the same unitary \(D^\ell(R)\), so their inner product is invariant.

Kakarala's theorem does not cover this case. The sphere is a space that SO(3) acts on, not the group itself, so its coefficients are vectors, and a theorem about invertible matrices doesn't apply (Kondor, 2007). We come back to completeness on the sphere below.

This is the bottom model of the figure at the top. Its input is a descriptor of 254 invariants of the digit at \(L = 12\), mostly triple products of this kind. An MLP trained on upright digits only reads the spinning 2 as a 2 in every pose, and it is right on 95.7% of upright test digits and 95.7% of rotated ones.

Rebuilding the Earth

Invariance is easy to check by rotating the signal. Completeness, the claim that nothing but the pose is lost, can be tested by rebuilding a signal from its invariants alone. Here is the Earth's land mask in spherical harmonics up to degree 20, or 441 coefficients. Its power spectrum is 21 numbers, and random globes with the same 21 look nothing like the Earth. From the 638 invariants in the lower panel, sums of products of three or four coefficients, a numerical solver rebuilds the land mask in a pose of its own. Rotated onto the original, it matches to about 12 digits.

How selective bispectra reduce complexity

The cost of the full bispectrum

The full bispectrum has an entry for every pair of irreps, so on a finite group it holds \(O(|G|^2)\) scalars, quadratic in the size of the signal. A 32 × 32 image under cyclic shifts has 1,024 pixels and a full bispectrum of 524,800 entries. At 256 × 256 the FFT gives 65,536 coefficients and the bispectrum 2,147,516,416 entries, about 17 GB at 8 bytes each, per image and per forward pass. That size is a large part of why networks have rarely used a seventy-year-old invariant.

A chain on the ring

Most of those entries are redundant. On the ring, \(c_0\) is real for a real signal, so \(\beta_{0,0} = c_0^3\) gives \(c_0\) and \(\beta_{0,1} = c_0 |c_1|^2\) gives \(|c_1|\). Each entry \(\beta_{1,k} = c_1 c_k \overline{c_{k+1}}\) then gives the next coefficient,

\[ c_{k+1} = \overline{\beta_{1,k} / (c_1 c_k)}. \]

Step 4 of the ring figure runs this chain. No entry fixes the phase of \(c_1\), and step 5 shows that any value of it gives the signal back, rotated. For a generic signal no division is by zero, so on a ring of \(n\) points these \(n\) of the \(n(n+1)/2\) distinct entries determine the signal up to its pose (Giannakis, 1989), and every other entry can be recomputed from them.

A selective bispectrum is a subset of this kind, enough entries to determine a generic signal up to the group action, with a count linear in the size of the signal. For the 256 × 256 image that is 65,536 numbers, half a megabyte. Mataigne et al. (2024) select \(O(|G|)\) entries on any finite group by a search over its table of tensor products, like the \(D_5\) table in the fold-out. For the 24 rotations of a cube they keep 172 of the 576 scalars.

The sphere, degree by degree

On the sphere the full bispectrum grows more slowly, as \(L^3\) for \((L + 1)^2\) coefficients, but each entry is a sum of Clebsch–Gordan terms. At \(L = 15\), the setting of the paper's experiments, a signal has 256 coefficients and the full bispectrum 1,124 entries. At \(L = 64\) its 70,257 entries add up 267 million such terms. Our selective version keeps 380 numbers at \(L = 15\) and 5,610 at \(L = 64\), for 25 times less work. Counting repeated full entries once (352 and 22,945), the selective version is no smaller at \(L = 15\) and 8 times smaller at \(L = 64\).

The sphere has no chain as simple as the ring's, since each degree brings \(2\ell + 1\) unknowns at once, so the construction goes degree by degree. Once the lower degrees are known, an entry \(\beta_{a, b, \ell}\) with \(a, b < \ell\) is linear in \(\overline{\mathbf F_\ell}\). Each degree keeps up to \(2\ell + 1\) of these, plus a few self-couplings \(\beta_{\ell, \ell, \ell'}\) and product energies \(\|(\mathbf F_{\ell_1} \otimes \mathbf F_{\ell_2})|_\ell\|^2\), chosen so that the system for \(\mathbf F_\ell\) has full rank. The total is \(\Theta(L^2)\), and the paper calls the result the augmented selective spherical bispectrum.

Completeness of selective bispectra is proven for commutative groups, the dihedral groups and the rotations of a cube (Mataigne et al., 2024), and for images on the disk (Myers Lantow and Miolane, 2026). On the sphere it is a conjecture. The count is enough (254 numbers at \(L = 12\) against the 166 needed), the Jacobian at each degree up to \(\ell = 100\) has full rank at random signals, \(\beta_{2,3,4}\) tells a signal from its mirror image, and the solver recovered all 32 test signals at \(L = 15\) up to a rotation. The Jacobian check is local, so it can't rule out a distant signal with the same invariants.

Some experiments and results

Spherical MNIST

The classifiers at the top are small versions of the paper's main experiment, Spherical MNIST (Cohen et al., 2018), with digits on a 64 × 128 spherical grid, training on upright digits, testing on rotated ones, and five seeds. Our model takes the selective bispectrum at \(L = 15\) (380 complex numbers), applies \(\mathrm{sign}(x) \log(1 + |x|)\) to the real and imaginary parts, and feeds the 760 channels to a three-layer MLP. It is compared with a spherical CNN and an e3nn tensor-product network matched in size, and with two controls, the power spectrum with an MLP and a planar CNN.

The selective bispectrum with an MLP reaches 95.8% on rotated digits. Both equivariant networks reach 96.1%, a 0.3-point lead that is within noise for the spherical CNN (\(p = 0.14\)) and significant for the tensor-product network (\(p = 0.006\)). Like the tensor-product network, our model scores the same on rotated test digits as on upright ones, where the spherical CNN drops from 97.1% to 96.1%. The planar CNN falls to 13.4%. The power spectrum is just as invariant and reaches 77.7%, and an MLP 48 times larger lifts it only to 80.1%, so the gap comes from what the features keep, not from the size of the head.

Two caveats. With less training data the spherical CNN, which learns its features, leads at every training-set size below the full one, by 13.0 points at 100 examples, while our model still beats the tensor-product network up to 2,500. And an invariant model can't tell a 6 from an upside-down 9. Of the 426 mistakes that the small model at the top makes on the 10,000 test digits, 117 are a 6 and a 9 taken for each other. If the label depends on orientation, use a smaller group or keep the pose as a feature.

Where the speed comes from

Our model takes 0.26 ms per batch, 4 times less than the spherical CNN and 364 times less than the tensor-product network. The architectures explain most of it. Both equivariant networks do their group theory in every layer, the spherical CNN with learned convolutions over rotations and the tensor-product network with Clebsch–Gordan products of learned features. Our model does it once, in a fixed layer without weights, and passes 760 real numbers to a plain MLP.

Three implementation choices keep that layer cheap. Selectivity makes the number of entries, and the work to compute them, grow like the signal rather than its square. Precomputation moves the group theory to construction time. Every module stores its Clebsch–Gordan matrices and Fourier kernels as buffers, and on the sphere it also lists every nonzero Clebsch–Gordan term of every kept entry once, cached on disk, so the forward pass is a gather, a product and a scatter-add over one flat table. And at batch 16 a forward pass is a few dozen small kernels, whose wall-clock is set by the host launching them more than by the GPU. With autograd off, each module records its forward pass as a CUDA graph on the first input and replays it as one launch for every later input of the same shape.

Use cases

The paper uses the bispectrum in three ways, as a pooling layer, as a set of invariant features, and through its inverse.

Pooling. In a group-equivariant CNN, the bispectrum can replace the max over poses. Across two networks and up to five alternatives, 46 comparisons in all, it is never significantly worse. On 3D organ scans under the cube's 24 rotations (OrganMNIST3D), it lifts accuracy from 73.6% with max pooling to 78.5% on the same backbone, and its smallest model (0.46M parameters) beats max pooling's largest (6.0M) by 2.6 points. The gain disappears with 250 training examples or fewer and is not significant at the largest width. On tissue images under 8 rotations (PatchCamelyon) everything ties, in tests that three seeds per model make sensitive only to gaps of about 0.025 AUC.

Invariant features. With no weights to learn, the bispectrum can be the whole feature extractor, straight into an MLP, as in the Spherical MNIST model. It is a strong default when the symmetry is known and the training set is not small.

Inversion. Six of the seven modules map a selective bispectrum back to a signal, in a pose of its own, which can then be aligned with the original. That makes it possible to average or compare signals seen in unknown poses. On the sphere, SO3onS2.invert raises NotImplementedError, and the solver behind this page's figures lives in the paper's experiment code, not in the library.

Try it with PyTorch

bispectrum installs with pip install bispectrum on Python 3.12 and depends on PyTorch, NumPy and torch-harmonics. There is one module per tile of the group figure, each a torch.nn.Module without parameters, named {Group}on{Domain}. Calling a module returns its selective bispectrum, and invert maps it back. The modules are differentiable, so they can sit anywhere in a network. Here is the swap from the talk, in a group-equivariant CNN with 8 rotations. 

# pip install bispectrum
from bispectrum import CnonCn

# x: group conv output, (B, C, 8 poses, H, W)
bsp = CnonCn(n=8)
rings = x.movedim(2, -1).reshape(-1, 8)
beta = bsp(rings)  # instead of x.max(dim=2)

Each of the \(B \cdot C \cdot H \cdot W\) rings of 8 poses comes back as 8 complex invariants where the max kept 1 real number. To feed the next layer, the paper splits them into real and imaginary parts, compresses them with a signed log, and mixes them back down to \(C\) channels with a 1 × 1 convolution, 

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from bispectrum import CnonCn

# x: output of a group convolution, (batch, channels, 8 poses, height, width)
before = x.max(dim=2).values               # keep the largest of the 8

bsp = CnonCn(n=8)                          # keep everything but the pose
B, C, G, H, W = x.shape
# one ring of 8 per channel and pixel
beta = bsp(x.permute(0, 1, 3, 4, 2).reshape(-1, G))
z = torch.cat([beta.real, beta.imag], dim=-1)
z = z.sign() * z.abs().log1p()
z = z.reshape(B, C, H, W, -1).permute(0, 1, 4, 2, 3).reshape(B, -1, H, W)
after = F.relu(proj(z))                    # proj = nn.Conv2d(16 * C, C, 1)

Put the ReLU after the first learned projection, as here. Placed before it, a ReLU zeroes every negative invariant, which costs 4 to 5 points on OrganMNIST3D. For the groups where it exists, selective=False returns the full bispectrum instead, which for a 32 × 32 image runs to hundreds of thousands of entries. DnonDn, SO2onDisk and OctaonOcta only have the selective one. In the benchmark below the selective modules return 76 to 1,024 numbers per signal.

Every module in the table takes under a millisecond with autograd on, from 0.07 to 0.67 ms, and 0.03 to 0.20 ms at inference.

In your browser

The figures on this page run the same math in TypeScript, at \(L = 12\). Draw on the pad below (clear the 0 first). When you lift the pen, the page computes the 254 invariants of your drawing and gives only those to the solver. Very symmetric drawings, such as a dot or a centered ring, are unchanged by every rotation about some axis and so far from generic. If the solver gives up, press Reconstruct for other random starts.

Conclusion

For decades the bispectrum has been an exact invariant, complete for generic signals on many groups, and too big to use in a network. Selective bispectra make it linear in the size of the signal, and bispectrum makes it one PyTorch module per symmetry, under a millisecond per batch. We hope you will use it in your own projects, as a pooling layer in an equivariant network, as fixed invariant features in front of a small model, or to compare and align signals seen in unknown poses. Two questions are still open, a proof of completeness on the sphere and inversion on the sphere inside the library, and we welcome issues and pull requests.
